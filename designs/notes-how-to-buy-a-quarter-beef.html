How to buy a quarter beef (and what 110 pounds actually looks like in your freezer)

Bulk beef is the best deal in local food and the most confusing first purchase. Here's the math, the vocabulary, and the questions that separate a great rancher from a rough one.

Hanging weight vs. take-home weight

Most ranches price a quarter by hanging weight — the carcass after slaughter, before it's cut. Take-home weight is what actually goes in your freezer, and it's roughly 60–65% of hanging weight after bone, trim and moisture loss during dry-aging. So a quarter listed at 175 lb hanging comes home as about 110 lb of packaged beef. Some ranches quote a flat take-home price instead; either is fine, but make sure you know which one you're looking at before you compare.

Rule of thumb: 1 cubic foot of chest freezer holds 25–30 lb of packaged beef. Check your freezer before you reserve.

The price per pound you're really paying

Take the total and divide by take-home pounds. A quarter at $1,650 for ~110 lb take-home is about $15/lb — for a mix that includes ribeyes and tenderloin, not just ground. Compare that to buying the same cuts individually at the same farm stand and a quarter usually lands 25–35% cheaper. Ask whether processing is included; on some ranches it's a separate $150–250 paid to the butcher at pickup.

The cut sheet, decoded

After you reserve, the butcher sends a cut sheet: how thick you want your steaks, how many per package, how much goes to ground, whether you want soup bones, organ meats and the tallow. If you've never filled one out, tell the rancher — every good one will walk you through it on the phone in ten minutes. Two defaults that rarely disappoint: 1-inch steaks, two per pack, and 1-lb ground packages.

Five questions to ask before you put down a deposit

  1. Grass-finished or grain-finished? Both are fine; they taste different. Just know which you're getting.
  2. Which processor, and are they USDA-inspected? Custom-exempt processing is legal for shares in most states but has different rules.
  3. Hanging or take-home pricing? See above.
  4. When's harvest and when's pickup? Fall reservations often pick up 4–8 weeks after harvest. You'll need the freezer empty by then.
  5. Can I see the pasture? A rancher who says "come Saturday" is the one you want.

Where to find them
